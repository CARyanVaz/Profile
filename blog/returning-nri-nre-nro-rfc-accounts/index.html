Returning to India? When to convert your NRE and NRO accounts to resident or RFC accounts

Your bank accounts follow FEMA, not the 182-day income tax test. If you return to work, run a business or settle in India, your NRE and NRO accounts must be redesignated from the day you arrive, and the choice between resident and RFC accounts decides how much interest stays tax-free during your RNOR years.

By Ryan Vaz, FCA · 20 October 2026 · 12 min read

The short answer

You must redesignate your NRE and NRO accounts the moment you become a "person resident in India" under FEMA. For anyone returning to take up a job, start a business or settle down, that is the day you land, not 182 days later. NRO balances move to an ordinary resident savings account. NRE balances move either to a resident savings account or, at your option, to a Resident Foreign Currency (RFC) account.

Most returning NRIs get this wrong in one of two ways. Some keep the NRE account running for months because they think they are "still NRI for tax". Others rush everything into rupees and lose both currency flexibility and the tax exemption available on RFC interest during the RNOR years.

This guide is for anyone moving back to India: salaried professionals returning from the Gulf, the US, the UK or East Africa, retirees coming home, and families relocating for children's education or ageing parents. It explains when the change is triggered, what converts into what, how the tax works, and the exact steps to take with your bank.

Two points of confusion are worth clearing up early. First, an RFC account is not the same as an RFC(D) account. RFC(D) is a domestic foreign currency account for residents who bring back foreign exchange from travel or receive small foreign earnings; it is not the returning-NRI product discussed here. Second, an RFC account cannot be opened while you are still an NRI. Banks will ask for proof that you have returned, usually your passport arrival stamp and a declaration of intention to stay.

When exactly the conversion is triggered

The trigger is your residential status under FEMA, not under the Income-tax Act. The two laws define residence differently, and the bank account rules follow FEMA alone.

Under Section 2(v) of FEMA, you become a person resident in India immediately when you come to or stay in India for any of these reasons:

  • to take up employment or carry on a business or vocation in India; or
  • in circumstances showing an intention to stay in India for an uncertain period.

There is no waiting period. If you land in Mumbai on 15 November having resigned your job in Dubai, you are a FEMA resident from 15 November. RBI's deposit rules say NRE balances should be redesignated or moved to RFC immediately upon return for employment or on change in residential status.

The income-tax test is different. Under Section 6 of the Income-tax Act, 1961 (and its counterpart in the Income-tax Act, 2025, which applies from Tax Year 2026-27), residence is based on days spent in India in the financial or tax year. A person who returns in November may still be a non-resident for that year's income tax, while already being a resident under FEMA.

Visits do not trigger conversion. If you come home for a wedding, a holiday or medical treatment and intend to go back, you remain a non-resident under FEMA and your NRE and NRO accounts continue as they are. The question is always why you are in India, not how long.

NRO money cannot go into an RFC account. NRO balances are already rupees earned in India, so the only destination is an ordinary resident account. Only NRE and FCNR(B) balances, and foreign-currency assets you held abroad, qualify for RFC.

One caution on NRE fixed deposits. If you break an NRE FD to move it into RFC before it has completed one year, some banks pay interest only at the RFC rate, or the applicable savings rate, rather than the contracted rate. Check the bank's premature-withdrawal terms before you choose.

In practice most banks keep the same account number when they redesignate NRE or NRO to resident. Standing instructions, UPI handles and ECS mandates usually continue, but confirm this in writing with the branch.

The RFC account in detail

An RFC account lets a returning NRI keep foreign earnings in foreign currency, as a resident, with no restriction on how the money is used. It is the single most useful tool for anyone who has built up dollar, pound or euro savings abroad.

Who can open one

You must be a returning NRI or PIO who was resident outside India for a continuous period of at least one year before returning, and who has now become a resident. Banks such as SVC Bank list these conditions on their RFC pages. A joint RFC account is usually permitted only with another returning NRI, or a resident relative on a former-or-survivor basis, depending on the bank.

What money can go in

  • balances in your NRE and FCNR(B) accounts at the time of return;
  • foreign exchange you earned through employment, business or vocation abroad while you were a non-resident;
  • proceeds from selling assets held abroad, such as shares, property or a foreign bank balance;
  • pension, superannuation or other monetary benefits from an employer outside India;
  • foreign exchange received as gift or inheritance from a person resident outside India;
  • insurance claims or maturity proceeds from an insurer outside India.

What the money can be used for

RFC balances carry no restriction on use. You can remit them abroad for any bona fide purpose without RBI approval, invest abroad, pay foreign school fees, or convert to rupees whenever you need to spend in India, as Dhanlaxmi Bank's RFC terms summarise. Because RFC funds are your pre-return foreign assets, these remittances do not count against your LRS limit of USD 250,000 a year.

Currencies and formats

RFC accounts can be held as savings, current or term deposits in freely convertible currencies such as USD, GBP, EUR, JPY, AUD and CAD. Term deposits typically run from six months to three years. Many banks do not offer GCC currencies, so Gulf returnees usually hold USD.

If you go abroad again

An RFC account can be redesignated back to NRE or FCNR(B) once you become a non-resident again, so the money never has to come into rupees at all.

The tax angle: why RFC usually wins during the RNOR years

NRE interest is exempt only while you are a non-resident under FEMA. That exemption under Section 10(4)(ii) of the 1961 Act ends on the day you become a FEMA resident, even if you are still non-resident or RNOR for income tax. Continuing to run the account does not keep the interest tax-free; it only means the bank is not deducting TDS on income that is now taxable.

RFC and FCNR interest are treated differently. Interest on RFC deposits and on FCNR(B) deposits you continue to hold is exempt under Section 10(15)(iv)(fa) for as long as you are Resident but Not Ordinarily Resident (RNOR). Once you become Resident and Ordinarily Resident (ROR), it becomes taxable.

Some online guides say redesignated NRE FDs stay tax-free during RNOR. That reading is not safe. The prevailing professional view, reflected in CA Surekha Ahuja's analysis and in replies by BCAS past president Ameet Patel on Moneylife, is that RNOR status alone does not preserve the NRE exemption. To keep interest exempt in the RNOR years, the money should sit in RFC or in an existing FCNR(B) deposit.

Who qualifies as RNOR

Under Section 6(6) of the 1961 Act, a resident individual is RNOR for a year if either of these is true:

  • they were a non-resident in 9 of the 10 preceding years; or
  • they spent 729 days or fewer in India in the 7 preceding years.

Someone who has lived abroad for a decade or more typically enjoys 2 to 3 years of RNOR status after return. During those years, foreign income such as rent from a flat abroad, foreign dividends and foreign retirement accounts generally stays outside Indian tax, as long as it does not arise from a business controlled from India. See also who is an NRI for Indian income tax.

The Income-tax Act, 2025

For income earned from 1 April 2026 (Tax Year 2026-27 onwards), the same exemptions and the RNOR test continue under the new Act with renumbered provisions. Section numbers above refer to the 1961 Act, which still governs income up to 31 March 2026. Cite the correct Act for the year in question in your return and in any reply to the department.

A worked example

Anita returns to Mumbai on 1 December 2026 after 14 years in Singapore. She holds ₹40 lakh in an NRE savings account, an NRE FD of ₹25 lakh maturing in June 2027, and an FCNR(B) deposit of USD 60,000 maturing in March 2028.

  1. On 1 December 2026 she becomes a FEMA resident. She tells her bank the same week.
  2. She opens an RFC USD account and moves the NRE savings balance into it. Interest there is exempt while she is RNOR.
  3. The NRE FD is redesignated as a resident FD at its contracted rate. Interest accruing after 1 December 2026 is taxable.
  4. The FCNR(B) deposit runs to March 2028. Its interest stays exempt while she is RNOR, which in her case likely covers both the 2026-27 and 2027-28 tax years.
  5. At FCNR maturity she rolls the proceeds into an RFC term deposit, or converts to rupees if she needs the money in India.

Step-by-step: converting your accounts

  1. Decide your plan before you fly. List every NRE, NRO and FCNR account and FD, with balances and maturity dates. Decide which foreign-currency money you want to keep in foreign currency.
  2. Inform each bank in writing within days of arrival. Most banks have a "change of residential status" form; some accept a request through net banking. Give your date of return.
  3. Ask for NRE and NRO savings to be redesignated as resident savings. Confirm whether the account number, debit card and mandates continue.
  4. Open the RFC account at the same time, in the currency you want, and instruct the bank to transfer the NRE savings balance into it. For FCNR(B), give a maturity instruction to credit RFC or a resident rupee account.
  5. Handle fixed deposits separately. Instruct the bank whether each NRE FD should be redesignated as a resident FD till maturity or moved to RFC, after checking any premature-withdrawal penalty.
  6. Update everything linked to the old status: demat and trading accounts (close PIS and NRE/NRO demat, open resident demat), mutual fund folios, insurance policies, PPF, NPS and your KYC records.
  7. Update the bank's FATCA/CRS self-certification to show India as your tax residence.
  8. Keep a file of every request and acknowledgement, together with your passport arrival stamp, for income-tax and FEMA records.

Documents usually asked for

  • passport with the arrival stamp, and visa or residence-permit cancellation if available;
  • PAN card and updated Aadhaar;
  • proof of Indian address (Aadhaar, utility bill or rental agreement);
  • the bank's residential-status change form and RFC account-opening form;
  • a declaration of your date of return and intention to stay in India;
  • recent photographs and your FATCA/CRS declaration;
  • for RFC credits from assets abroad: proof of the asset sale, pension or gift, and the foreign bank statement showing the remittance.

What happens if you do not convert, and mistakes to avoid

Holding an NRE or NRO account after becoming a FEMA resident is a contravention of the FEMA deposit regulations. Under Section 13 of FEMA, the penalty can go up to three times the amount involved, or up to ₹2 lakh where the amount cannot be quantified, with a further daily penalty for continuing default. Contraventions can be compounded with RBI, but compounding involves an application, a fee and time. It is far cheaper to convert on time.

The tax exposure is usually larger than the FEMA one. Interest wrongly treated as exempt must be offered to tax with interest under Sections 234B and 234C. If it surfaces through AIS or a bank's revised reporting, it can lead to a notice and, in some cases, penalty for under-reporting.

Mistakes we see most often

  • assuming you remain an NRI until you have spent 182 days in India;
  • believing RNOR status keeps NRE interest tax-free;
  • moving all foreign savings into rupees on arrival, losing currency flexibility and the RFC exemption;
  • crediting new foreign income to the redesignated old NRE account instead of RFC;
  • breaking NRE FDs early to move into RFC without checking the penalty and interest rate;
  • forgetting PIS, NRE/NRO demat, mutual fund and insurance records, which also carry non-resident status;
  • not keeping proof of the date of return, which decides both the FEMA and the tax position.

In closing

Convert when you become a FEMA resident, and that is usually the day you return. Send NRO to resident savings, choose between resident savings and RFC for NRE, and hold FCNR(B) to maturity. Plan these moves around your RNOR window, and you keep both the law and the tax exemption on your side.

Common questions

Can I keep my NRE account if I plan to go back abroad in two years?

If you have returned to take up a job or settle for an uncertain period, no. You become a FEMA resident on arrival. Use an RFC account; it can be converted back to NRE or FCNR(B) when you leave.

Can I open an RFC account from abroad before returning?

No. The account can only be opened once you are a resident, and banks ask for proof of arrival.

Is there a limit on how much I can keep in RFC?

There is no upper limit on eligible credits. The limit is on what can be credited, not on the amount.

Can I add new foreign income to RFC after I return?

Income from foreign assets you held when you were a non-resident, such as rent, dividends, pension or sale proceeds, can generally be credited. Fresh earnings from work done after you became resident usually go to a resident account. Check with your bank.

Does the bank convert my accounts automatically?

Banks do not know your status has changed unless you tell them. The obligation to inform them is yours.

What about GIFT City deposits?

Deposits with IFSC banking units in GIFT City are a separate option worth discussing with your adviser, especially for planning beyond the RNOR years.
