Who is an NRI for Indian income tax? Residential status explained

Whether you are taxed in India on worldwide income or only on Indian income depends on your residential status for the tax year. It is decided by days in India, not by passport, visa or bank account. Written by Ryan Vaz, FCA, a Fellow Chartered Accountant in Borivali West, Mumbai.

Why residential status matters

Whether you are taxed in India on worldwide income or only on Indian income depends on your residential status for the tax year. It is decided by days of stay, not by passport, visa or the type of bank account you hold. The rules are in section 6 of the Income-tax Act, 2025, which keeps the same section number as the 1961 Act. From 1 April 2026 the period is called the tax year (1 April to 31 March).

Residential status for income tax is separate from status under foreign exchange law (FEMA), which governs bank accounts and investments. A person can be non-resident for FEMA and resident for income tax, or the other way round, so both should be checked. For bank accounts after returning, see NRE, NRO and RFC accounts after returning to India.

The basic tests

An individual is resident in India for a tax year if either of the following is true:

  • they were in India for 182 days or more during the year, or
  • they were in India for 60 days or more during the year and 365 days or more in total during the four preceding years.

Anyone who meets neither test is non-resident. Days are counted from passport stamps or travel records; the day of arrival and the day of departure are usually both counted as days in India.

Exceptions to the 60-day test

The 60-day test does not apply, and 182 days is used instead, for two groups:

  • Indian citizens who leave India during the year for employment abroad, or as crew of an Indian ship
  • Indian citizens and persons of Indian origin who live abroad and come on a visit to India, if their Indian income (excluding income from foreign sources) is ₹15 lakh or less.

If a visiting Indian citizen or person of Indian origin has Indian income above ₹15 lakh, the 60 days becomes 120 days. They are resident if they stay 120 days or more in the year and 365 days or more in the four preceding years. Someone who becomes resident only through this 120-day rule is treated as Resident but Not Ordinarily Resident (RNOR).

Deemed residency

An Indian citizen whose Indian income exceeds ₹15 lakh, and who is not liable to tax in any other country because of domicile or residence, is deemed to be resident in India whatever the number of days. A deemed resident is treated as RNOR.

RNOR: the status that matters for returning Indians

A resident is either ordinarily resident or not ordinarily resident (RNOR). An RNOR is taxed in India on Indian income, and on foreign income only if it comes from a business controlled in, or a profession set up in, India. Other foreign income, such as salary earned abroad before return, interest on foreign deposits and foreign rent, is not taxed in India in RNOR years.

A resident individual is RNOR if either of the following applies:

  • they were non-resident in nine out of the ten preceding years, or
  • they were in India for 729 days or less in total during the seven preceding years.

For someone returning after a decade abroad, this usually means one to three years of RNOR status, which is time to reorganise foreign assets and accounts before full residency begins.

Anita, an Indian citizen working in Dubai since 2014, visits Mumbai each year. Her only Indian income is rent and NRO interest of about ₹6 lakh a year.

What depends on the status

Status decides what income is taxed, which return form and schedules are used, whether foreign assets and foreign income must be reported, and whether treaty relief is available. A missed residency can mean undeclared foreign income; an assumed residency can mean paying Indian tax on income that was not taxable here. For anyone near the thresholds, a day count kept through the year is the simplest protection. More questions are answered on NRI tax questions.

Questions on NRI residential status

How many days can an NRI stay in India without becoming resident?

For most visiting Indian citizens and persons of Indian origin whose Indian income is ₹15 lakh or less, the limit is under 182 days in the tax year. If Indian income exceeds ₹15 lakh, staying 120 days or more makes them resident if they also spent 365 days or more in India over the preceding four years.

What is the 120-day rule for NRIs?

It applies to Indian citizens and persons of Indian origin visiting India whose Indian income exceeds ₹15 lakh. They become resident if they are in India for 120 days or more in the tax year and 365 days or more in the four preceding years. Anyone who is resident only because of this rule is treated as RNOR.

Who is a deemed resident of India?

An Indian citizen whose Indian income exceeds ₹15 lakh and who is not liable to tax in any other country because of domicile or residence is deemed resident in India, whatever the number of days. A deemed resident is treated as Resident but Not Ordinarily Resident.

What is RNOR status and how long does it last?

RNOR (Resident but Not Ordinarily Resident) applies to a resident who was non-resident in nine of the ten preceding years, or was in India for 729 days or less in the seven preceding years. Foreign income, other than from a business controlled or profession set up in India, is not taxed in India in RNOR years. For someone returning after about ten years abroad, it usually lasts one to three years.

Is residential status for income tax the same as under FEMA?

No. Income tax status is based on days of stay in the tax year. FEMA status depends on the purpose and intention of the stay, and it governs bank accounts and investments. Both should be checked, as they decide different things.

How are days in India counted for residential status?

Days are counted from passport stamps or travel records for the tax year, 1 April to 31 March. The day of arrival and the day of departure are usually both counted as days in India, so a careful count is needed when you are close to a threshold.

Does citizenship or passport decide residential status?

No. Residential status depends on days in India, not on citizenship or passport. Citizenship matters only for the exceptions: the relaxed 182-day and 120-day rules apply to Indian citizens and persons of Indian origin, and deemed residency applies only to Indian citizens.

What changes in my return when my status changes?

A non-resident reports only Indian income. A resident and ordinarily resident reports worldwide income and must disclose foreign assets and foreign income. An RNOR reports Indian income and foreign income from a business or profession controlled in India. The return form, the schedules and the availability of treaty relief follow from the status.
